Negromancy.

What relations from the past still shape life now? How do they become visible? What would have to change for a different response to become possible?

Starting position

The past does not simply survive. It is carried, altered, and made active through particular relations.

This is a working proposition, not a fact established by one scholar. The investigation begins in Jamaica, with histories of enslavement, resistance, law, language and performance.

01 · Classification

A word becomes a crime.

In 1760, colonial Jamaica criminalized Obeah after the uprising known as Tacky’s Revolt. The law treated the practice as dangerous. That classification shaped what officials could punish and what later records would describe.

Supported history

Katharine Gerbner documents the criminalization and examines the practices that colonial records compressed under the term. Diana Paton traces how Obeah’s legal and political meanings changed across the Caribbean.

Gerbner, 2025 · Paton, 2015

Negromantic reading

A legal category can do more than describe a practice. It can change the conditions under which that practice can be recognized, transmitted, defended or suppressed.

Unproven question

Which capacities were displaced by the classification, and which reappeared under other names? This cannot be answered by treating every later resemblance as direct survival.

02 · The record

Who kept the evidence?

Many records of slavery were produced by the people and institutions enforcing it. Reading them remains necessary. Treating them as complete would be a mistake.

Scholarship

Saidiya Hartman examines the violence and limits of the archive in “Venus in Two Acts.” Gerbner studies moments when the perspective of Afro-Jamaicans interrupts a colonial account.

Hartman, 2008 · Gerbner, 2025 (open access)

Interpretation

Absence is itself something to investigate: who was able to record, preserve, classify, and publish an account?

Speculation

Can a silence be approached through performance or ritual without claiming to recover the unrecorded thoughts of the dead?

03 · Recognition

A body learns a world.

People are shaped by institutions, language, expectations and experience. Some relations become ordinary enough to seem natural.

Scholarship

Sylvia Wynter examines historically specific genres of the human. Frantz Fanon writes about racialized embodiment and language. Diana Taylor studies the transmission of knowledge through performance; M. Jacqui Alexander connects memory, colonial power and the sacred.

Wynter, 2003 · Fanon, 1952 / trans. 2008 · Taylor, 2003 · Alexander, 2005/2006

Negromantic reading

A role can organize what a person is permitted, expected, or able to do. Rehearsal can test alternate responses—but it does not by itself undo material constraints.

Open question

Could historical consciousness be practiced as an embodied capacity rather than treated only as knowledge of past events? What would count as a test?

A recognizable role is not the whole person performing it.

04 · Consequence

Who bears the cost?

An arrangement can appear stable because someone carries its cost. That cost may be bodily, financial, emotional, political or ecological.

Grounding

Deborah A. Thomas examines violence, witnessing and repair in contemporary Jamaica. Christina Sharpe studies the ongoing conditions she calls the “wake” of slavery. Neither work proves a single universal theory of bearing.

Thomas, 2019 · Sharpe, 2016

Negromantic proposal

To understand an institution or relationship, ask what must be spent to keep it working—and who has the power to shift that burden.

Research gap

When does a change repair a harmful arrangement, and when does it merely transfer the cost to someone else?

05 · At the limit

What cannot yet be shown?

Negromancy includes intuition, ritual and spiritual speculation. They can generate questions and inform the significance of an experience. They do not establish an empirical cause without appropriate evidence.

Working concepts

Returned relation: a proposal about relations displaced or declared finished that become active in changed conditions. Fetish-space-time: a proposed lens for studying how produced relations appear as fixed properties of bodies, objects, places or times. Both require precise case studies.

Deliberately speculative

Could ritual cultivate forms of attention unavailable to an ordinary documentary account? What could be observed? What must remain an interpretation of sacred experience rather than a causal finding?

Not knowing is not evidence of a hidden cause. It is a reason to ask a better question.

Research practice

How to investigate a relation.

01 / Locate

Start with an event, document, practice, object or first-person account. Name the place and period.

02 / Trace

Identify institutions, bodies, classifications, material conditions, and routes of transmission. Follow primary sources and scholarship.

03 / Distinguish

Keep the source’s claim, the investigator’s interpretation, and speculative extensions in separate records.

04 / Test

Compare rival explanations. Look for counterexamples, missing sources, and places where the reading fails.

05 / Respond

Ask whether the investigation changes an actual judgment, action, or practice—and who bears the consequences.

Make this a shared investigation.

Choose one contested classification in a Jamaican archive. Show the source, identify what it can and cannot establish, and propose a test of your interpretation. Human and AI contributions are welcome; disagreement counts.
