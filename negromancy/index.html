The Practice / Philosophical ground / Reader 01

What remains
active?

Negromancy is a developing Black-Caribbean philosophy and practice of historical relation, mediation, consciousness, performance and transformation.

Its question is not merely what happened in the past. It asks how relations made through history continue to organize bodies, institutions, knowledge and possibilities of life—and how different responses might become practicable.

01 / The governing problem

History is not over simply because an event has ended.

A person encounters the world through a body, a language, inherited knowledge, institutions, resources and expectations. These arrangements make some relations perceptible and actionable while obscuring others. They also shape what responses appear possible and which costs must be carried.

The October 2026 manuscript develops the argument from the Caribbean history of colonial conquest, enslavement, the plantation, racial classification, cultural survival and transformation. The Caribbean is not merely one setting in which an already complete philosophy is applied; it is among the conditions from which this inquiry emerges.

What is still acting?

Identify a historically produced relation and trace how it operates in a specific present encounter.

How is it recognized?

Examine bodies, knowledge, mediations and authorities that make the relation intelligible—or difficult to hear.

What would have to change?

Distinguish understanding a relation from cultivating and sustaining a materially different response.

Editorial basis: Negromancy to Date: First Theoretical Reconstruction, 20 October 2026, §§I–XVII, read alongside the 7 October conceptual and scholarly audit. The manuscript is explicitly provisional, not an authorized final statement. This page paraphrases its active arguments; it is not a quotation or a replacement for it.

02 / Concepts in active use

An argument, not a cabinet of terms.

These twelve introductions follow the manuscript from historically constituted relations through recognition, embodiment, witnessing and repair. The names are not interchangeable: each identifies a particular problem. Every introduction links directly to scholars whose work can clarify, complicate or challenge it.

Only active conceptual questions are presented here. A supporting scholar contributes a relevant argument, not independent validation of Negromancy's larger synthesis.

01 / Manuscript §IIResearch field ↓

Relation and inhabitable worlds

A world is not an isolated object, nor is the individual a completed being who merely enters it. Negromancy examines the historically organized relations through which people become intelligible, acquire capacities, encounter limits and act. A school, plantation, household, theatre or state can organize a particular field of life without becoming a separate universe. Relations can sustain care or domination; their existence does not make them harmonious.

Scholarly relation
Sylvia Wynter · Édouard Glissant · Katherine McKittrick

What the work must establish. Choose a specific scene and demonstrate the connections among institutions, bodies, materials and authority. Name the mechanism; do not use “relation” as an all-purpose explanation.

02 / Manuscript §§III–IVResearch field ↓

Historical constitution and returned relation

The Caribbean is an intellectual ground, not a decorative example: conquest, enslavement, plantation economies, spiritual persecution and racialized administration severed and reorganized relations. Historical continuity is neither total erasure nor intact survival. What seems past may remain active through a law, role, repertoire, land arrangement or inherited expectation. Return occurs under changed circumstances; it is not restoration of an original world.

Scholarly relation
Deborah A. Thomas · Michel-Rolph Trouillot · Katharine Gerbner · Christina Sharpe

What the work must establish. Trace a relation across dated evidence. Similar appearances across eras do not, alone, demonstrate one continuous mechanism.

03 / Manuscript §§IV–VResearch field ↓

Historical consciousness, casting and embodied futures

History inhabits bodies in gesture, language, fear, skill, status, expectation and the material possibilities ahead of them. Historical consciousness is not simply remembering earlier events: it is a cultivated capacity to recognize and investigate how one participates in relations shaping the present and the future. Casting names the expectations of a role; capture names the conditions that make departure difficult. Recognition may enlarge possibility, but it does not dissolve dependency.

Scholarly relation
Frantz Fanon · Sylvia Wynter · Diana Taylor · Sonjah Stanley Niaah

What the work must establish. Distinguish a felt recognition from evidence of its historical causes; distinguish the possibility of imagining change from the resources needed to sustain it.

04 / Manuscript §VIResearch field ↓

Mediation: from perception to authority

Not everything that exists is immediately perceptible, and not everything perceived becomes legible or authoritative. Language, a legal category, a ritual, photograph, archive, scientific instrument or performance can mediate an encounter. Some mediations also change the relation itself: an official designation, for example, may alter eligibility or permission. The vital distinction is between the existence of a relation, the manner in which it is recognized, and the power to act upon it.

Scholarly relation
M. Jacqui Alexander · Birgit Meyer · Webb Keane · Sylvia Wynter

What the work must establish. Identify the precise medium and the institutional procedure; show when recognition merely describes and when it materially changes an outcome.

05 / Manuscript §VI; historical questionResearch field ↓

Who may authorize knowledge and practice?

Colonial law did more than prohibit acts: it asserted authority to classify spiritual practices, prescribe which knowledge was credible and regulate whose capacities could be exercised. The criminalization of Obeah provides a bounded historical test. A legal category reveals what a governing institution claimed authority over; it does not exhaust the practices, intentions and histories of those it named.

Scholarly relation
Diana Paton · Katharine Gerbner · J. Brent Crosson · Deborah A. Thomas

What the work must establish. Use precise statutes, dates, enforcement records and practitioner accounts. Do not infer a single uninterrupted genealogy across unrelated jurisdictions.

06 / Manuscript §VIIIResearch field ↓

Circulation and bearing

A household, economy or institution may appear stable because its labour and costs are carried unequally. Bearing investigates what must be expended or endured for a relation to continue: time, money, care, bodily exposure, ecological risk, credibility, shame and lost future capacity. The visible coherence of an arrangement says little, by itself, about its justice or sustainability. History is active not only in inherited meanings but in the ongoing distribution of maintenance and possibility.

Scholarly relation
George L. Beckford · Deborah A. Thomas · Joan C. Tronto · Iris Marion Young

What the work must establish. Document who performs the work, who gains, what is displaced and how the consequences accumulate over time.

07 / Manuscript §§VIII, XIIIResearch field ↓

Conversion, responsibility and repair

A pressure can be redirected into another form without changing the conditions that produce it. The manuscript calls attention to this difference: a survival strategy may keep someone alive while leaving the harmful arrangement intact; a policy may name a problem without redistributing its costs. Repair asks whether resources, authority, protection, time or viable alternatives actually change. Even a redistribution can transfer burdens to another vulnerable person, so its consequences must be tested.

Scholarly relation
Deborah A. Thomas · Joan C. Tronto · Iris Marion Young · Christina Sharpe

What the work must establish. Specify the harm, the intervention, whose bearing changes and what recurs. An apology, recognition or eloquent account is not by itself evidence of repair.

08 / Manuscript §IXResearch field ↓

Mystery, opacity and sacred relations

Negromancy treats lived sacred experience as a serious subject, not as an embarrassing residue to be explained away. But experience, interpretation and claims of external causation are distinct. Mystery names disciplined inquiry at a boundary of explanation. Opacity, indebted especially to Glissant, concerns the limit of another's demand for full knowledge or possession. Neither requires that evidence be abandoned; neither authorizes the theory to decide every spiritual question in advance.

Scholarly relation
M. Jacqui Alexander · Édouard Glissant · J. Brent Crosson · Saidiya Hartman

What the work must establish. Ask what happened, how a participant understood it, what practice accompanied it, which effects were observed and which claims remain unresolved or protected.

09 / Manuscript §XResearch field ↓

Witnessing, testimony and the politics of reception

To encounter a relation is not the same as being able to give it form; giving testimony is not the same as being heard. A witness speaks from a position, with interests, limits and responsibilities. Negromancy examines how institutions and publics learn to receive some speech as reason, evidence or authority and dismiss other speech in advance. Testimony can alter a relation, but it cannot certify every inference made from the experience it records.

Scholarly relation
Deborah A. Thomas · Saidiya Hartman · Kamau Brathwaite · Carolyn Cooper

What the work must establish. Preserve original words, provenance and conditions of reception; separate what a witness saw from what the record can corroborate.

10 / Manuscript §XIResearch field ↓

Repertoire, rehearsal and the capacity to respond

Repertoires are embodied practices acquired through repetition—gestures, roles, rituals, forms of care, techniques of obedience and refusal. They can reproduce power as readily as resist it. Rehearsal matters because different responses can be tried within altered circumstances. An actor can feel a possible movement before they can sustain it in life. Negromancy therefore asks what resources, protections, relationships and conditions allow a newly rehearsed capacity to become effective.

Scholarly relation
Diana Taylor · Joseph Roach · Sonjah Stanley Niaah · Frantz Fanon

What the work must establish. Study a particular practice, not “performance” in the abstract. Show what was repeated, altered, learned and sustained—and what remained impossible.

11 / Manuscript §XIIResearch field ↓

Conjuration, Sankofa and the practice of return

The name invokes a relation to the dead, yet not every return to the past is an act of literal spirit invocation. Archives, poems, scenes, oral testimony, ritual and public investigation can make historically active but obscured relations available for encounter. Sankofa orients return toward the possibilities of the future, not a fantasy of pristine recovery. Conjuration asks how a relation is brought into presence, what can legitimately be claimed of it, and how the response becomes answerable.

Scholarly relation
M. Jacqui Alexander · Diana Taylor · Saidiya Hartman · Joseph Roach

What the work must establish. Keep documentary trace, creative representation, embodied effect and spiritual interpretation as distinct records. The dead cannot be compelled to speak for a theory.

12 / Manuscript §§XIII–XVIIResearch field ↓

Negromancy as philosophy and answerable practice

These questions do not form a doctrine that can be proven by combining appealing scholarship. Negromancy is an unfinished philosophical and practical inquiry: historical research, embodied investigation, writing, performance, spiritual practice, institutional analysis and repair. Its recurring demand is that explanations remain corrigible and that what becomes recognizable be examined for its consequences. Prodigal Humanism develops a narrower ethical question within this wider work.

Scholarly relation
Sylvia Wynter · Édouard Glissant · M. Jacqui Alexander · Deborah A. Thomas · Iris Marion Young

What the work must establish. For each claimed result, identify the source, the interpretation, possible opposing accounts, observed effects and the conditions under which the claim should be revised.

03 / Scholarly fields

The books have to be brought into relation.

The purpose of this library is not to announce a lineage of endorsements. It is to identify what each scholar investigates, where their argument can be used, and what their combined—even conflicting—methods make possible for a Black-Caribbean philosophy of historically active relation.

Field I / Scholarly conversation

Worlds, embodiment and the historically made subject

How does a world make people recognizable—and shape what they can become?

Wynter interrogates historically authorized definitions of the human; Glissant contests the imagined completeness of isolated beings; Fanon follows colonial categories into bodily experience; McKittrick shows how Black life actively produces geography. These inquiries do not collapse into one theory. Taken together, they make it possible to study institutions and places as conditions of subject formation rather than neutral backgrounds.

What this collection of scholarship makes possible. These works become a comparative research field in which disagreements and different kinds of evidence can refine Negromancy's claims.

Limit. The framework still has to establish which categories, places and practices caused which effects, in which period. Relation cannot replace explanation.

Concepts in conversation: Relation and inhabitable worlds · Historical constitution and returned relation · Historical consciousness, casting and embodied futures

Sylvia Wynter

Jamaican novelist and philosopher of the human

Work “Unsettling the Coloniality of Being/Power/Truth/Freedom” (2003); “Towards the Sociogenic Principle” (2001)

Wynter asks how one historically produced account of Man came to stand for the human as such. Her sociogenic approach joins human embodiment to the codes, institutions and stories through which lives become intelligible.

Negromancy's question. Foundational to questions of who authorizes the human, how categories constitute subjects, and what counts as intelligence.

Édouard Glissant

Martinican poet, novelist and philosopher of Relation

Work Poétique de la Relation (1990); Poetics of Relation (1997 English translation)

Glissant proposes that beings emerge through relationships rather than possess self-sufficient identities, while his right to opacity contests demands for total transparency. His Caribbean-grounded account also complicates any singular origin story.

Negromancy's question. Supports historically inhabited worlds and ethical limits on knowing; does not prove a universal mechanism behind every relation.

Publisher / source record ↗

Frantz Fanon

Martinican psychiatrist and anticolonial political thinker

Work Peau noire, masques blancs / Black Skin, White Masks (1952)

Fanon examines the racialized body as it encounters language, the gaze, institutions and colonial classification. The problem is not only ideological misdescription: social history becomes lived pressure and bodily orientation.

Negromancy's question. Supports embodied history, social roles and the distinction between recognizing an imposed role and escaping its constraints.

Katherine McKittrick

Black geographer and critic of racialized spatial knowledge

Work Demonic Grounds: Black Women and the Cartographies of Struggle (2006)

McKittrick shows how Black women's geographies are made within—and against—racialized arrangements of place. Her sites of inquiry are materially and historically specific rather than neutral containers of experience.

Negromancy's question. Helps make room, road, land, plantation and stage possible sites of theorizing instead of metaphors detached from material histories.

Publisher / source record ↗
Field II / Scholarly conversation

Caribbean historical production, colonial law and political authority

When a governing institution classifies a practice, what has it actually come to know?

Trouillot traces historical silencing; Thomas follows Jamaican sovereignty, witnessing and repair after the plantation; Gerbner and Paton reconstruct the changing colonial and postcolonial classifications of Obeah; Crosson puts living spiritual work into conversation with categories imposed upon it. Together they permit a historical investigation of the power to recognize and criminalize without confusing a colonial record with a complete practitioner history.

What this collection of scholarship makes possible. It furnishes distinct historical methods, concepts and tests for the questions above; the encounter between these works produces a research programme, not an already verified new theory.

Limit. An eighteenth-century law, a twentieth-century Jamaican state inquiry and a contemporary Trinidadian ethnography are different evidentiary objects. Connections require demonstrable transitions, not a seamless genealogy.

Concepts in conversation: Historical constitution and returned relation · Who may authorize knowledge and practice? · Witnessing, testimony and the politics of reception

Michel-Rolph Trouillot

Haitian anthropologist and historian of historical production

Work Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History (1995)

Trouillot traces power across the making of sources, archives, historical narratives and retrospective significance. What a society can describe as history depends partly on how its evidence was produced and preserved.

Negromancy's question. Makes archival silence a historical problem to investigate, not a blank permission to invent missing lives.

Publisher / source record ↗

Deborah A. Thomas

Anthropologist of Jamaica, sovereignty and witnessing

Work Political Life in the Wake of the Plantation: Sovereignty, Witnessing, Repair (2019)

Starting from the 2010 Tivoli Gardens incursion in Jamaica, Thomas joins plantation history, state violence, affect, visual and oral archives, and questions of accountability and repair. Her work insists on contemporary scenes, not an abstract history of domination.

Negromancy's question. Connects historical continuity, testimony and the practical limits of recognition without making the entire present a simple repetition of the plantation.

Publisher / source record ↗

Katharine Gerbner

Historian of slavery and Africana religious histories

Work Archival Irruptions: Constructing Religion and Criminalizing Obeah in Eighteenth-Century Jamaica (2025)

Gerbner investigates Obeah's entry into British colonial law in 1760, reading missionary records for moments when Africana worlds interrupt European archival categories. The label's later criminal status cannot define all the practices it enclosed.

Negromancy's question. A historically bounded test for how colonial institutions gained authority to classify religion, healing and spiritual practice.

Publisher / source record ↗

Diana Paton

Caribbean historian of slavery, emancipation and religion

Work The Cultural Politics of Obeah: Religion, Colonialism and Modernity in the Caribbean World (2015)

Paton follows the changing political uses of Obeah across slavery, emancipation, colonial regulation and postcolonial life. She studies both practice and representation, resisting the reduction of Obeah to a timeless definition.

Negromancy's question. Makes legal classifications an object of historical criticism, not an exhaustive account of those they designate.

Publisher / source record ↗

J. Brent Crosson

Anthropologist of religion and Caribbean spiritual work

Work Experiments with Power: Obeah and the Remaking of Religion in Trinidad (2020)

Crosson studies contemporary Caribbean spiritual work as practical experimentation with power, healing, justice and the limits of institutional definitions of religion. Trinidad's setting differs from eighteenth-century Jamaica.

Negromancy's question. Introduces living practitioner concepts and testable descriptions of ritual procedures that can correct conclusions drawn only from colonial archives.

Publisher / source record ↗

Also read George L. Beckford for political economy and M. Jacqui Alexander for sacred and colonial mediation.

Field III / Scholarly conversation

Sacred encounter, material mediation and the limits of knowledge

How do practices make relations perceptible without granting us possession of their causes?

Alexander treats sacred memory and crossing as historically situated modes of knowing; Meyer examines media and sensory presence; Keane studies the moral and semiotic orders that make signs and objects matter; Crosson asks how Caribbean spiritual workers define and assess their practices. Their combined scholarship provides tools for describing operations, participants, materials, experiences, criteria of efficacy and political authority separately.

What this collection of scholarship makes possible. These works become a comparative research field in which disagreements and different kinds of evidence can refine Negromancy's claims.

Limit. Respect for spiritual interpretation does not establish supernatural causation; secular explanation must not be used to erase participant knowledge. The forms of evidence remain distinct.

Concepts in conversation: Mediation: from perception to authority · Mystery, opacity and sacred relations

M. Jacqui Alexander

Caribbean feminist theorist of colonial power, sexuality and sacred memory

Work Pedagogies of Crossing: Meditations on Feminism, Sexual Politics, Memory, and the Sacred (2006 edition)

Alexander joins dangerous memory, colonial regulation, the sacred, embodiment and political pedagogy. Her chapter on making the invisible tangible challenges secular institutions' assumed monopoly on legitimate knowledge without detaching spiritual practice from material and political life.

Negromancy's question. Central to crossing, sacred relations and the conditions under which historically cultivated discernment becomes possible.

Publisher / source record ↗

Birgit Meyer

Anthropologist of religion, media and sensory forms

Work Sensational Movies: Video, Vision, and Christianity in Ghana (2015)

Meyer studies how images, media and bodily sensation organize religious presence and perception. Her Ghanaian materials show that what becomes perceptible depends on historically situated media and practices, not merely abstract belief.

Negromancy's question. Offers methods for asking what a practice makes present and how that presence is experienced, without predetermining supernatural causation.

Publisher / source record ↗

Webb Keane

Anthropologist of language, materiality and religion

Work Christian Moderns: Freedom and Fetish in the Mission Encounter (2007)

Keane examines Christianity, conversion and semiotic assumptions through a missionary encounter in Indonesia. His work shows how ideas of proper signs, objects, moral agency and modernity shape what actions mean.

Negromancy's question. Adds rigor to mediation: signs and material practices operate within particular social theories of what they can do.

Publisher / source record ↗

The historical controls here are Gerbner and Paton; living practitioner categories are further developed by Crosson.

Field IV / Scholarly conversation

Language, archive, witness and rehearsed performance

How does a relation move from living experience into testimony and alternative action?

Taylor distinguishes archive and embodied repertoire; Roach examines performance around loss; Hartman makes the ethics of archival silence inescapable; Sharpe joins afterlife and care. Brathwaite, Cooper and Stanley Niaah locate language, sound and performance in Caribbean historical life. Together they make it possible to treat speech, dance, gesture, staging, poetry and testimony as research materials—and to ask what rehearsal might alter.

What this collection of scholarship makes possible. It furnishes distinct historical methods, concepts and tests for the questions above; the encounter between these works produces a research programme, not an already verified new theory.

Limit. A powerful performance is not automatically a causal demonstration. Its audience, circumstances, limits and effects must be observed rather than imagined.

Diana Taylor

Scholar of theatre, performance and cultural memory

Work The Archive and the Repertoire: Performing Cultural Memory in the Americas (2003)

Taylor distinguishes documents and durable records from embodied repertoires transmitted through gesture, movement, sound, ritual and performance. Neither archive nor repertoire is outside power or free from selectivity.

Negromancy's question. Supports performance as a source and method of historical inquiry, with separate standards for observing and inferring.

Publisher / source record ↗

Joseph Roach

Scholar of circum-Atlantic performance and memory

Work Cities of the Dead: Circum-Atlantic Performance (1996)

Roach examines how performance, surrogation and public memory respond to loss across circum-Atlantic histories. Substitution can reorganize remembrance without recovering those who have been lost.

Negromancy's question. Places ethical limits on representing the dead and helps question what living performance transmits or changes.

Publisher / source record ↗

Saidiya Hartman

Historian and literary scholar of slavery, archives and Black life

Work “Venus in Two Acts” (2008); Scenes of Subjection (1997)

Hartman confronts the violence and silence of slavery's archive, developing disciplined ways of writing at the limits of available evidence. Her work questions both the archive's authority and the researcher's desire to complete it.

Negromancy's question. Protects absence from easy appropriation: an evocative story or performance does not entitle the researcher to make the dead speak.

Publisher / source record ↗

Christina Sharpe

Scholar of the continuing material and representational afterlives of slavery

Work In the Wake: On Blackness and Being (2016)

Sharpe uses wake, weather, ship and hold to describe ongoing conditions shaped by slavery and the practices of care, attention and witness that life amid those conditions demands.

Negromancy's question. Presses bearing beyond historical explanation toward sustained forms of care, while requiring the distinct history of each present scene.

Publisher / source record ↗

Kamau Brathwaite

Barbadian poet and historian of Caribbean cultural form

Work History of the Voice: The Development of Nation Language in Anglophone Caribbean Poetry (1984)

Brathwaite develops the significance of nation language, sound and Caribbean historical experience for literary form. His work challenges the assumption that sanctioned metropolitan language is the sole medium of intellectual authority.

Negromancy's question. Makes speech, rhythm, poetry and linguistic prestige central questions for witnessing and embodied historical practice.

Publisher / source record ↗

Carolyn Cooper

Jamaican literary and cultural critic

Work Noises in the Blood: Orality, Gender, and the “Vulgar” Body of Jamaican Popular Culture (1993)

Cooper examines oral, musical and literary forms—including Louise Bennett and Jamaican DJ culture—whose legitimacy has been contested through class, respectability and language.

Negromancy's question. Provides a Jamaican-grounded critique of who is allowed to sound knowledgeable, artistic or authoritative.

Publisher / source record ↗

Sonjah Stanley Niaah

Jamaican cultural geographer of dancehall

Work DanceHall: From Slave Ship to Ghetto (2010)

Stanley Niaah traces performance, place, migratory networks, spatial politics and dancehall's embodied and ritual dimensions. Dancehall emerges from particular Kingston relations, not generic entertainment.

Negromancy's question. Connects space and performed knowledge, and tests how institutional limits shape the conditions of artistic and social action.

Field V / Scholarly conversation

Plantation political economy, care and the conditions of repair

Who carries the work, loss and risk that keep an arrangement functioning?

Beckford locates persistent inequality in plantation political economy; Tronto makes care a political question of obligations and resources; Young considers responsibility for structural injustice; Thomas documents how witnessing and demands for repair confront institutions in Jamaica. Together they make it possible to move from saying that power is historically produced to investigating its material distribution: land, time, labour, money, infrastructure, ecological risk and future capacity.

What this collection of scholarship makes possible. It furnishes distinct historical methods, concepts and tests for the questions above; the encounter between these works produces a research programme, not an already verified new theory.

Limit. A cost ledger cannot by itself settle ethical conflict. Redress must be assessed over time, including burdens passed to others; recognizing injustice is not evidence that repair has occurred.

Concepts in conversation: Circulation and bearing · Conversion, responsibility and repair

George L. Beckford

Jamaican political economist of plantation underdevelopment

Work Persistent Poverty: Underdevelopment in Plantation Economies of the Third World (1972)

Beckford shows how plantation institutions and political-economic arrangements organize persistent inequality beyond formal changes in political status. His emphasis is structural and material rather than merely symbolic.

Negromancy's question. Grounds the question of bearing in land, labour, wealth, production, dependency and long-term maintenance costs.

Publisher / source record ↗

Joan C. Tronto

Political theorist of care, responsibility and democracy

Work Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care (1993)

Tronto treats care as a political and institutional problem, not only an individual virtue. Responsibility for maintenance, dependency and need is socially arranged and unequally distributed.

Negromancy's question. Helps distinguish the recognition of suffering from the conditions and obligations required to sustain a meaningful response.

Publisher / source record ↗

Iris Marion Young

Political philosopher of structural injustice

Work Responsibility for Justice (2011, posthumous)

Young's social-connection account asks how participants in structural processes bear political responsibility for injustice beyond cases of individually assigned blame.

Negromancy's question. Helps articulate responsibility for inherited arrangements without assuming that all participants caused or can repair their harms in the same way.

04 / What follows from the research fields?

From historical recognition to material consequence.

Together the fields permit a sequence of questions. They do not establish the answer to any particular case until the relevant historical or empirical evidence has been examined.

Identify the relation.

Begin with a bounded scene—legal classification, a performance, welfare assessment, hurricane warning or institutional decision. What was organized, by whom and when?

Trace the mediation.

Follow language, archive, embodied capacity, spiritual practice, technology and authority without treating every mechanism as equivalent.

Test the bearing.

Who must carry labour, risk, care, delay, ecological damage or future cost? What evidence would demonstrate that an intervention changes those conditions?

For Morrow: these questions connect the Black-Caribbean ground of the philosophy with artificial intelligence, climate action, finance, performance and the political authority of classification. They can generate specific investigations, but should never be treated as proof by analogy. This page is the philosophical ground; the Fieldbook collects bounded encounters, and the Insights Exchange opens them to independent criticism.

05 / Questions not yet answered

The sources must be allowed to correct the theory.

The October manuscript names unresolved tensions. They are retained as active pressure tests, not as a license to add speculative systems to the public conceptual spine.

01 / Relation versus general ontology

Studying historically mediated worlds does not by itself prove what all reality fundamentally is.

02 / Historical formation versus agency

How do new capacities arise when inherited circumstances shape the available repertoires of action?

03 / Mediation versus material causation

When does a new category change only interpretation, and when does it actually change a person's conditions?

04 / Opacity versus sufficient inquiry

How do justified limits on disclosure differ from refusal to substantiate a claim?

05 / Spiritual experience versus causal explanation

How can sacred practice be taken seriously without automatically certifying or dismissing metaphysical claims?

06 / Bearing versus repair

What constitutes a real change when a solution helps one group but transfers costs to another?

07 / Witness versus authority

Who can legitimately speak for a relation, and under what conditions can a representation be challenged?

08 / Caribbean ground versus general application

Which concepts can travel to other settings without erasing their origin or assuming universal mechanisms?

09 / Performance versus explanation

What does rehearsal demonstrate, and what remains a separate historical or material causal claim?

10 / The theory's own production

Can Negromancy apply its evidentiary and ethical demands to the institutions and technologies that give its own claims authority?
