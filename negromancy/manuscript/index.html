I. The governing problem

Negromancy begins from a problem of historical existence: how do relations produced in the past continue to organize the conditions of life in the present, particularly when those relations have been obscured, disavowed, naturalized, violently disrupted, or declared finished? But the problem extends beyond historical persistence. Human beings encounter the world through bodies, senses, languages, categories, institutions, inherited practices, technologies, memories, beliefs, and forms of recognition. These mediate how relations become perceptible and intelligible, what significance they acquire, which responses appear possible, and what consequences follow. A world does not become available to its inhabitants in its entirety. It is encountered through particular arrangements that make some relations recognizable while obscuring others. These arrangements are themselves historically constituted, materially sustained, and subject to transformation. Negromancy is concerned with the historical production and reproduction of these arrangements, their effects upon the beings who inhabit them, and the possibility of cultivating capacities to encounter and transform them. Its central concern might therefore be expressed through three interconnected questions: What relations are still acting here? How have we been made capable—or incapable—of perceiving, recognizing, inhabiting, and responding to them? What would have to change for different relations, embodiments, and futures to become possible? These questions identify Negromancy as more than an interpretive framework. They suggest an inquiry into the conditions under which histories become active, worlds become inhabitable, knowledge becomes authoritative, and transformation becomes practicable. The framework is also an ethical and practical undertaking. Its purpose is not exhausted by making relations intelligible. It asks what responsibilities follow from witnessing them. This is the provisional governing problem.

II. Relation and the historical constitution of worlds

Negromancy develops from an understanding of existence as relational, without treating relation as inherently harmonious, liberating, or even mutually recognizable. Here, Sylvia Wynter and Édouard Glissant provide two indispensable starting points. Wynter's account of the sociogenic principle makes possible an understanding of human subjectivity as constituted through biological existence and historically produced systems of meaning, classification, and institutional organization.

Her critique of the overrepresentation of a particular Western genre of Man challenges the tendency to mistake a historically specific description of the human for humanity itself. The consequences are substantial. What appears natural, rational, desirable, pathological, intelligent, or properly human may depend upon the historical organization of the categories through which persons become recognizable. Glissant's account of Relation challenges conceptions of beings as self-sufficient entities whose significance can be fully established independently of their encounters, histories, environments, and relations with others. Relation does not simply add context to already complete identities. It is constitutive of their emergence and transformation. Yet Glissant also insists upon opacity: the irreducibility of a person or relation to another's demand for transparency. Relations need not become completely knowable, classifiable, or possessable to be ethically or politically meaningful. Taken together, these approaches permit a foundational shift. The question is no longer only how historically produced classifications distort our understanding of a pre-existing humanity. It becomes how historically constituted relations participate in producing the very forms through which human existence is understood, experienced, organized, and enacted. Negromancy extends this inquiry into what may be called the historical constitution of inhabitable worlds. A world, in this usage, is not the universe as a total physical object. It is an historically organized field of relations within which beings live, become intelligible, acquire capacities, encounter constraints, and act. Worlds are constituted through material conditions and social processes, but also through patterns of interpretation, affect, memory, attachment, authority, practice, and anticipation. A family, school, workplace, plantation, theatre, church, legal order, market, or state does not constitute a separate universe. Each may nevertheless organize a particular field of relations through which the wider world becomes encounterable. These fields overlap, conflict, and transform one another. Consequently, a human being does not simply occupy an externally given world. The person is formed through participation in relations that make particular ways of inhabiting and responding to the world available. But this formation is never complete. No historically constituted order exhausts the relations that sustain it, the beings who inhabit it, or the possibilities of its transformation. This incompleteness is fundamental to Negromancy.

III. Colonial-racial detonation and the problem of historical continuity

The Caribbean is not an illustrative setting in which Negromancy applies an otherwise universal theory. It is a historical and intellectual ground from which the theory is developed. The violence of Atlantic enslavement, colonial conquest, plantation production, racial classification, dispossession, religious persecution, and subsequent systems of political and economic governance involved the violent reorganization of existing relations. Bodies were separated from lands, kinship networks, languages, political authorities, spiritual practices, forms of labour, and inherited possibilities of life. These disruptions did not simply eliminate earlier relations. They forced their recomposition under radically unequal conditions. The concept of colonial-racial detonation was developed to describe this violent disarticulation and forced reassembly of relational worlds. Detonation must not be confused with annihilation. African and Indigenous histories, practices, memories, languages, spiritual traditions, and political capacities were neither uniformly destroyed nor preserved unchanged. They persisted, transformed, crossed, re-emerged, and entered into new relations under historical pressures. Plantation society also produced novel relations: between enslaved Africans, colonial authorities, land, commodities, law, kinship, religion, language, markets, and emergent forms of collective life. These histories cannot adequately be represented as a single movement from original coherence through colonial destruction toward eventual restoration. Negromancy is interested instead in the historical processes by which relations are interrupted, reconstituted, transmitted, obscured, and made active again. This gives rise to returned relation. Returned relation names historically produced relations that have been displaced, obscured, violently disarticulated, declared finished, or made difficult to articulate, but which remain active or become newly active in subsequent encounters. Their activity may be observable through institutions, legal categories, landholding, economic arrangements, social roles, bodily orientations, speech, cultural repertoires, archives, ritual, or spiritual experience. The concept does not assume that every apparent recurrence is caused by the same historical process. The particular relations and mechanisms must be established. Nor does it require every return to be understood supernaturally. However, neither does Negromancy establish secular explanation as the only legitimate form through which people may interpret encounters with ancestral or sacred relations.

The crucial distinction is between the existence of an experience, the historical conditions of its possibility, the interpretations through which it becomes meaningful, and the claims that can be supported about its causes. Return is not restoration. What returns does so under changed circumstances. The relation may have been transformed, its carriers altered, its meaning contested, and its consequences redistributed. A returned relation is therefore not simply the past surviving into the present. It is a relation becoming active under historically particular conditions. This leads to the problem of historical consciousness.

IV. Historical consciousness: the embodied past and embodied future

Negromancy's developing conception of historical consciousness requires a reconsideration of what is meant by history. History cannot be restricted to a sequence of completed events, nor to the documentary record of those events. Historical processes also persist through the constitution of bodies, orientations, expectations, capacities, institutions, and possible futures. A body carries history without necessarily possessing conscious knowledge of the events and processes through which it was formed. The embodied past may appear through language, gesture, posture, skill, fear, desire, attachment, habit, social position, inherited material conditions, and repeated responses. The embodied future concerns the capacities, expectations, commitments, resources, vulnerabilities, and possible consequences already being organized through present existence. The future is not already completed inside the body. Rather, present conditions make different futures more or less reachable. A person who repeatedly sacrifices sleep, health, financial security, or emotional capacity to sustain an existing arrangement is not simply responding to present circumstances. That person may also be altering the conditions of future action. Likewise, a child whose environment supports curiosity and experimentation may acquire capacities that enlarge the range of possible responses available later in life. These examples indicate that history is active not only as remembered or inherited past, but also in the ongoing formation of future possibility. The present is therefore a site at which inherited relations are reproduced, contested, interrupted, and transformed. Historical consciousness, in this framework, may be understood as:

A cultivated embodied capacity to recognize, investigate, and respond to the historical relations through which present experience and future possibility are being constituted.

Historical consciousness is not equivalent to historical knowledge, although historical research can substantially develop it. A person may possess extensive factual knowledge of colonial history without recognizing how historically produced relations organize contemporary habits, institutions, or expectations. Conversely, a person may experience a powerful recognition of historical relation without possessing sufficient evidence to explain what has occurred. The two conditions must not be confused. Embodied recognition can initiate historical inquiry. Historical inquiry can correct embodied interpretation. Subsequent encounters can reorganize both. The relation between experience and research is therefore recursive. Negromancy asks not only what the subject knows about history, but how historical relations participate in producing the subject's capacities for knowing, feeling, interpreting, and acting. This is one of the strongest theoretical developments in the project. It begins to move historical consciousness from knowledge of history toward consciousness of one's participation within ongoing historical processes.

V. Casting, capture, and the performed constitution of subjectivity

The earlier conception that the character is a world provides a practical entrance into this problem. An actor does not adequately inhabit a character by identifying a fixed personality and reproducing its externally recognizable traits. Character emerges through a relation among circumstances, motivations, language, inherited experiences, other characters, dramatic action, bodily capacities, institutional conditions, and the requirements of performance. A character becomes performable through a world of relations. A similar process can be observed in social existence. People are cast into roles through historically organized expectations concerning gender, class, race, family, sexuality, profession, respectability, religion, citizenship, and other social positions. Casting names the process through which a relation establishes or anticipates what a body is expected to become and perform. It does not necessarily require a deliberate individual casting agent. An institution, classification, family arrangement, professional convention, or inherited repertoire may establish expectations that precede the particular body's participation.

Capture concerns the conditions that make alternative embodiment difficult to sustain. A person might recognize that an assigned role is harmful yet remain dependent upon the relationships, resources, protections, attachments, or forms of recognition that require its continued performance. To recognize that one is performing a role is therefore not necessarily to acquire the capacity to leave it. This distinction is central to Negromancy's conception of freedom. The subject is neither an entirely sovereign author of identity nor a passive product of structure. The subject composes responses within historically produced arrangements of possibility and constraint. This is the problem of crossing. Crossing describes the process through which beings encounter and compose responses amid intersecting historical, material, affective, institutional, and potentially sacred relations. The response may reproduce inherited arrangements, accommodate pressure, refuse an expectation, displace a burden, or generate a different relation. Performance is significant because it makes these processes accessible to deliberate practice. An actor can investigate the circumstances of a role, enter its relations, rehearse its possible responses, encounter unexpected bodily experiences, and revise the resulting performance. The actor does not possess complete sovereignty over the character. The character emerges through a process involving research, direction, other performers, physical conditions, rehearsal, and audience reception. Nevertheless, the actor participates in the coordination through which that character becomes embodied. This has important consequences for thinking about social identity. If social roles are also historically produced and repeatedly performed, then the apparent unity of a person's social character need not exhaust the embodied subject's capacity for alternative relations. The actorly insight is not that social existence is fake. It is that the recognizable coherence of a performed identity should not be mistaken for the totality of the being who performs it. Negromancy thereby identifies a potential distinction between the body as historically conditioned performer and the characters through which it has become socially recognizable. That distinction should not be converted into a doctrine of an untouched, authentic self existing beneath all history. The actor, too, is historically formed. What rehearsal may disclose is not an unconditioned essence, but capacities that an established role has not exhausted. This is why performance becomes methodologically important. It permits relations to be encountered, embodied, varied, and tested under altered conditions.

VI. Mediation: the production of visibility, recognition, and authority

A major theoretical development becomes possible when the preceding arguments are considered together. If embodied experience is historically constituted, if relation exceeds fixed identity, and if worlds are encountered through historically organized forms, then perception and recognition cannot be treated as entirely transparent operations. To perceive a relation is not necessarily to recognize what it is. To recognize something is not necessarily to understand the conditions through which it became recognizable. And to understand or describe a relation is not necessarily to possess the authority or capacity to act upon it. These distinctions require a more developed account of mediation. Mediation, provisionally, names the processes, relations, forms, and arrangements through which something becomes perceptible, intelligible, recognizable, communicable, authoritative, or actionable. A language can mediate experience. So can a ritual, scientific instrument, legal category, photograph, theatrical performance, archive, school curriculum, economic classification, or religious institution. Mediation is not simply a barrier between an observer and an independently complete reality. The mediating arrangement may participate in changing the relations it makes perceptible. A legal declaration can establish a recognized status. An institutional classification can change access to resources. A public accusation can reorganize relationships. A ritual may transform participants' experiences and obligations. A performance may produce a new understanding of a previously familiar situation. Different mediations operate through different mechanisms. Their causal effects cannot be assumed equivalent. Nevertheless, they share a theoretical problem: the relationship between how something becomes recognizable and what that recognition permits, prevents, or produces. Negromancy has increasingly approached this through the question of who or what is authorized to mediate reality and its meaning. Colonial institutions did not merely govern material resources. They also attempted to regulate the legitimacy of languages, spiritual practices, forms of knowledge, identities, and authorities. The historical criminalization of Obeah offers one particularly important field of investigation. Colonial legal authority could classify certain African-derived spiritual practices as illegitimate, fraudulent, or dangerous, while simultaneously enforcing its own jurisdiction over which forms of power and knowledge would be recognized.

But the legal classification of a practice does not exhaust the relations through which its practitioners understood, transmitted, or exercised authority. This was already apparent in the earlier Zion inquiry concerning the tension between the enslaved person classified as pieza and the alternative jurisdiction suggested by oath-bound relations. The enslaved body could be rendered legible through colonial classifications of property while participating in relations whose authority was not exhausted by plantation recognition. Those distinct jurisdictions might coexist in conflict within the same historical encounter. This suggests that power is exercised not only by determining what people may do, but also through historically organized conditions of recognition: who may speak, what may count as knowledge, which forms of authority are legitimate, and what kinds of relations can be acted upon. Yet recognition is not the same as existence. A relation may remain materially active even when a governing institution cannot or will not recognize it. Negromancy must therefore distinguish the existence of a relation from its legibility, authorization, and effects. Otherwise, the theory risks granting classificatory power the very sovereignty it seeks to question.

VII. Fetish-space-time: when produced relations appear self-evident

The concept of fetish-space-time provides a means of investigating the appearance of historically constituted relations as apparently independent realities. Its intellectual genealogy includes the Atlantic history of the concept of the fetish, Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism, and broader critiques of reification and naturalized social forms. Negromancy's development of the concept has sought to investigate how relations produced through historical processes become condensed into objects, identities, roles, institutions, places, and temporal classifications that appear self-sufficient. An object may appear to possess value independently of the relations through which that value was produced and recognized. An institution may appear to possess authority independently of the historical arrangements through which its jurisdiction was established. A social identity may appear natural despite its dependence upon particular histories of classification, regulation, embodiment, and reception. A place may appear separated from relations that historically produced its landholding, infrastructure, cultural meaning, or political organization. A period may be declared completed even while relations constituted during that period continue to organize present conditions.

Fetish-space-time names a field in which such historically constituted forms acquire the appearance of self-evident existence. The concept does not imply that objects, institutions, or categories are unreal. Rather, it questions the apparent independence of their properties and authority from the relations that constitute and sustain them. It also raises questions concerning temporal and spatial organization. What is established as the past? What is recognized as the present? Which histories become bounded within apparently completed periods? What relations disappear when an object is isolated from the spatial and temporal processes through which it emerged? These are historical questions, not grounds for asserting that space and time are illusions. Fetish-space-time is strongest as a diagnostic concept. It becomes weak if every object, category, institution, or form of coherence is declared fetishistic. The task is to identify particular cases in which a produced relation is misrecognized as an autonomous property or self-evident order, and to explain how that misrecognition is sustained. This places the concept in direct relation to historical consciousness. If fetish-space-time concerns the forms through which historical relations become naturalized or obscured, historical consciousness concerns the cultivation of capacities to recognize and investigate those relations. However, making them recognizable does not automatically transform them. For that, we must return to circulation and bearing.

VIII. Circulation, bearing, and the maintenance of worlds

The earlier Zion framework contributed a particularly important insight: the coherence of an existing world may depend upon an unequal distribution of the labour and costs required to sustain it. Social arrangements persist through circulation. Resources, labour, money, authority, care, credibility, obligations, injuries, expectations, and affect move through relations that sustain institutions and forms of life. These circulations are not uniformly reciprocal or equitable. Some people and institutions possess greater capacity to direct them, while others are required to absorb their consequences. Bearing names what beings and arrangements must carry, accommodate, maintain, expend, or undergo for a particular relation or world to continue. Bearing may concern labour, time, risk, physical exposure, financial cost, care, credibility, shame, injury, responsibility, or depleted future capacity. The central question is not merely whether a social arrangement functions.

It is how that functioning becomes possible and where its costs fall. A household may appear stable because one member absorbs an unsustainable burden. An organization may appear efficient because workers perform uncompensated labour. A national economy may exhibit particular indicators of stability while critical costs are displaced onto households, public infrastructure, ecological systems, or future generations. These examples require empirical examination. The concept alone establishes none of their specific causes. But bearing provides a different criterion for analyzing apparent coherence. It insists that the continued functioning of an arrangement is not sufficient evidence of its legitimacy, sustainability, or justice. Zion also developed the problem of conversion. Conversion occurs when pressure is transformed or rerouted into another form without substantially changing the underlying distribution of bearing. A survival strategy may make an arrangement tolerable while perpetuating the conditions that make survival difficult. An institutional reform may make suffering more visible without materially reducing it. A new category may improve recognition while leaving the affected population responsible for the same burdens. Conversion is not necessarily ineffective or morally blameworthy. It may preserve life under severe constraint. But its success in maintaining an existing arrangement should not be confused with repairing that arrangement. This produces a crucial distinction:

Conversion reorganizes the form in which pressure is carried. Repair changes the conditions and distribution of its bearing.

Even this requires qualification. Not every redistribution is reparative. Burdens can be transferred from one vulnerable party to another. A solution may improve present conditions by consuming capacities required for future survival. The historical horizon of bearing therefore matters. A credible assessment must ask not only who benefits and who pays now, but how present interventions change capacities and obligations over time. Here, the earlier Zion inquiry into circulation joins the later Negromantic inquiry into embodied history. A world is not sustained by meaning alone. It is maintained through historically organized distributions of material and embodied capacity.

IX. The sacred, spirit, and the limits of secular interpretation

Negromancy cannot be adequately reconstructed as a purely secular theory that incorporates spirituality only as cultural representation. M. Jacqui Alexander's Pedagogies of Crossing has been especially important in developing this dimension. Alexander's account of sacred memory, embodied spiritual knowledge, crossing, and the political conditions of spiritual practice challenges the assumption that modern secular institutions possess an uncontested jurisdiction over what counts as legitimate knowledge. This challenge matters historically. Colonial authority organized hierarchies among religions, spiritual practices, bodies, sexualities, and forms of knowledge. The distinction between legitimate religion and illegitimate spiritual practice was itself implicated in political and material domination. Negromancy therefore takes seriously the possibility that spiritual and ritual practices mediate relations through which participants cultivate memory, obligation, recognition, authority, and transformative capacity. But the theory must carefully distinguish several questions. What did a participant experience? Through what historical and spiritual traditions was that experience understood? What practices, material arrangements, and embodied processes accompanied it? What observable changes followed? What claims about spiritual causation are being made, and what could establish or limit them? These questions cannot be answered by assuming that spiritual interpretation is always false. Neither can they be answered by assuming that spiritual interpretation is always correct. The developing conception of Mystery attempts to preserve inquiry at precisely this boundary. Mystery does not mean that an unexplained event confirms supernatural causation. It names a disciplined openness to relations whose causes, significance, or consequences are not yet exhaustively understood. Opacity performs a different function. Opacity limits the claim that a person, relation, historical subject, sacred practice, or experience must become completely legible in order to be legitimate or enter ethical relation. Mystery concerns the limits and openness of explanation. Opacity concerns the limits of possession. They may intersect, but they are not interchangeable.

Negromancy's more recent inquiry into the mediation of capacities to recognize and witness the sacred introduces a further question. What must a being be capable of sensing, discerning, remembering, performing, or sustaining for something to become recognizable as sacred? And how are those capacities historically cultivated, authorized, suppressed, or transformed? This question is not yet resolved. It should not be answered by treating the sacred as an invisible universal substance behind all relations. Nor should it be reduced to the claim that whatever a community designates sacred is thereby exhaustively explained. The more promising inquiry concerns the historically situated processes through which sacred relations are encountered, interpreted, instituted, contested, and acted upon. This would preserve spiritual seriousness without surrendering the distinctions required for disciplined inquiry.

X. Witnessing, articulation, and the politics of reception

Recognizing a relation does not guarantee that it can be communicated. Communicating an experience does not guarantee that it will be recognized as knowledge. And recognition does not necessarily produce an appropriate response. These difficulties have increasingly drawn Negromancy toward articulation, testimony, and reception. Relational articulation concerns the processes through which an encounter or relation acquires audible, sensible, legible, or performable form. Articulation does not simply reproduce an already completed interior experience. The act of giving form to something may change the speaker's relation to it and alter the conditions under which others can respond. Writing, speaking, performing, ritual action, and artistic arrangement may therefore participate in the ongoing constitution of relations. Testimony involves making something borne or witnessed available through an address that remains answerable to what can legitimately be claimed. It need not require exhaustive self-disclosure. Nor does truthful testimony guarantee that the speaker is innocent, that an interpretation is correct, or that the listener must accept every conclusion. The listener, too, participates in a historically organized relation. This produced the auxiliary conception of Hardears Reason.

Hardears Reason concerns the socially trained organization of receptivity through which some expressions become recognizable as reason, authority, evidence, or knowledge, while others are dismissed in advance as noise, ignorance, vulgarity, madness, superstition, or irrelevance. The concept asks how a listener's social role, institutional position, expectations, and learned interpretive repertoire condition reception. It should not be mistaken for a claim that all disagreement results from prejudice or failed listening. Rather, it invites inquiry into how prior classifications and expectations organize what a listener is prepared to recognize. Rehearing would then involve practices through which those conditions of reception can be altered. This has consequences beyond interpersonal communication. A public institution may recognize certain kinds of testimony only when they conform to established documentary, professional, linguistic, or procedural forms. A historical archive may preserve the language of the governing authority while excluding the interior lives and interpretive practices of those governed. A performance may become intelligible to one public while remaining illegible or threatening to another. Negromancy asks how these conditions of reception are produced and how they might be changed without assuming that every relation must surrender its opacity. The fundamental issue is not simply the right to speak. It is the organization of the capacities and authority through which speech, testimony, performance, and other forms of articulation become receivable.

XI. Repertoire, rehearsal, and the possibility of another response

The relationship between historical constitution and transformation becomes especially concrete through performance. Diana Taylor's account of the repertoire and Joseph Roach's work on circum-Atlantic performance establish important precedents for understanding embodied practices as forms of historical transmission. Negromancy draws upon these approaches while developing a particular interest in rehearsed response. Repertoire names forms of embodied response that have become available through repetition, training, memory, institutional participation, and prior encounters. These may include speech, posture, gesture, emotional regulation, ritual practice, learned obedience, refusal, collective action, improvisation, or artistic technique. A repertoire is not necessarily conscious.

A person may repeatedly enact a response without recognizing the historical processes through which it became available. Nor is a repertoire necessarily liberating. People can become highly skilled in practices that reproduce domination, dependency, or violence. Rehearsal concerns the repetition, experimentation, and adjustment through which responses become available for future enactment. In theatre, the distinction is readily observable. An actor can rehearse alternative ways of delivering a line, approaching a character, responding to another performer, or organizing a scene. The resulting changes may alter the relation among performers, audience, character, and text. But alternative embodiment is not achieved merely by imagining another response. It requires conditions under which that response can be practised, sustained, and made consequential. This distinction becomes more difficult outside theatre. A person may rehearse an alternative way of participating in a relationship yet encounter material dependence, social punishment, or institutional conditions that make the change difficult to maintain. Negromancy must therefore distinguish the capacity to imagine an alternative, the capacity to rehearse it, and the conditions required to enact and sustain it. This has been a continuing problem since TCIW and Bearing-Centered Inhabitation Analysis. The central concern is not simply that people should be free to perform alternative identities. It is that the conditions under which different embodiments become possible are unevenly distributed. Rehearsal may expose those conditions. It does not automatically overcome them. The practice becomes reparative only when its consequences alter the relations through which harmful casting and coerced bearing are reproduced. This establishes a relationship between historical consciousness and the cultivation of freedom. Freedom cannot be reduced to recognizing one's historical determination. It involves the development and support of capacities through which different responses become practicable.

XII. Conjuration, return, and the recasting of history

The word Negromancy invokes necromancy, but its developing theoretical meaning is not reducible to communication with the dead. The dead enter the inquiry through several distinct but related problems. There are people whose lives and relations have ended but whose consequences remain historically active.

There are relations violently severed or rendered inaccessible by domination. There are archives that preserve certain traces while destroying, obscuring, or excluding others. There are embodied repertoires whose origins may exceed the conscious knowledge of those who enact them. There are spiritual practices through which participants understand themselves to encounter ancestors or other sacred presences. And there are unrealized possibilities whose historical exclusion continues to affect the present. These are not all the same kind of existence. Negromancy becomes more rigorous by distinguishing them rather than collapsing them under an unrestricted language of haunting. The important development is the problem of double casting. The living cast the dead through archives, stories, ritual, historical interpretation, representation, and performance. At the same time, historically produced relations associated with the dead may continue to cast the living into roles, obligations, identities, expectations, and repertoires. This reciprocity is not necessarily symmetrical. The mechanisms differ. A living historian can construct a representation of a deceased person through documentary evidence. An institution established generations earlier may continue to organize the material possibilities of living people. A ritual practitioner may experience ancestral authority as active in the present. The claims being made in these cases require different kinds of evidence and interpretation. Nevertheless, each raises questions about the relations through which living and dead become historically consequential to one another. Conjuration, within the developing framework, may therefore be understood as a situated practice of bringing historically active but obscured relations into perceptible, interpretable, or performable form. This need not imply that all conjuration is literal spirit invocation. Equally, the existence of non-spiritual applications does not invalidate spiritual practice as a distinct field. The methodological question concerns how a relation is being made present, what claims are being made about that presence, and what consequences follow. Here, Sankofa remains significant as a practice of return that is oriented toward future possibility. Returning to history is not simply recovering an original state. It is an inquiry into relations whose consequences persist, whose representation remains incomplete, and whose transformation may require renewed encounter. Negromanscription offers a related practical possibility: the deliberate inscription of historically produced relations into forms that permit further interpretation, performance, revision, and transmission.

An archive, scene, character, poem, or testimony may become a site at which a relation can be re- entered under altered conditions. Such re-entry does not grant the practitioner sovereignty over the relation. The past cannot be made to disclose everything. The dead cannot be required to speak. Historical reconstruction remains accountable to evidence, absence, and the limits of representation. Negromancy thus treats return as an encounter with unfinished relation rather than recovery of a completely available past.

XIII. Repair, responsibility, and the cultivation of the bearer

The ethical horizon of Negromancy has increasingly concerned the transformation of the conditions through which relations are sustained. It does not follow that every relation should be preserved. Some relations are coercive, destructive, or dependent upon forms of bearing that cannot be ethically justified by their continued functioning. Others may require protection from forms of institutional intervention that would destroy the conditions of their existence. Still others may require transformation rather than preservation or elimination. The distinction between conversion and repair becomes necessary here. Repair involves an answerable transformation of the conditions through which harmful or coerced bearing is reproduced. It cannot be established by the intensity of an experience, the sincerity of an intention, or the elegance of its interpretation. Nor is repair equivalent to restoring an imagined original harmony. It may require redistributing resources, changing authority, providing protection, altering institutions, creating time, supporting care, widening access, or making refusal possible. The question is whether an intervention changes the conditions under which particular forms of suffering, dependency, or constrained response would otherwise recur. An important normative commitment inherited from Zion is that the apparent coherence of a world cannot, by itself, justify the sacrifice required to sustain it. But Negromancy must be careful not to confuse its ethical commitments with empirical descriptions. A claim that an arrangement imposes unequal bearing requires investigation. A claim that the distribution is unjust requires ethical and political argument. A claim that a particular intervention repairs the arrangement requires evidence of consequential change. These are related but distinct claims.

The emerging conception of the bearer introduces a further dimension. Bearing is not merely passive endurance. A being may cultivate the capacity to recognize what is being carried, distinguish obligations, coordinate responses, remain answerable to conscience, and transform its participation in inherited relations. This is not an injunction for the burdened to become better at suffering. Indeed, such an injunction could reproduce the very arrangement Negromancy seeks to challenge. The cultivation of the bearer must involve attention both to embodied capacity and to the conditions that make its exercise sustainable. Conscience enters here as a discipline of answerability. It does not automatically establish truth, nor does it exempt a person from the consequences of error. Rather, it concerns the ongoing responsibility to examine the relations one enters, the claims one makes, the burdens one accepts or transfers, and the effects of one's actions upon others. The developing relationship among hope, faith, belief, will, conscience, and testimony belongs to this inquiry, but its exact philosophical status remains unsettled. Its significance, at present, is that Negromancy has been approaching ethics not simply as a set of principles imposed upon action, but as a cultivated coordination of embodied capacities through repeated encounters. The bearer would therefore be understood not as a perfected moral subject, but as a being engaged in the unfinished practice of becoming answerable to relations and their consequences.

XIV. Negromancy as philosophy and practice

Negromancy cannot be reduced to a vocabulary for identifying hidden structures. Its development has repeatedly returned to practices through which historically produced relations become encounterable. These include: • Historical research and genealogical investigation; • Observation and documentation of embodied experience; • Performance, rehearsal, and acting; • Writing, poetry, narration, and testimony; • Listening and the investigation of reception; • Ritual, spiritual practice, and contemplative inquiry; • Analysis of institutions, material conditions, and distributions of bearing; • Comparative investigation and experimentation with alternative arrangements. These practices do not share identical standards of evidence or validation.

Historical documentation does not establish spiritual causation. Theatrical performance does not establish a universal account of subjectivity. Statistical association does not itself establish an ethical obligation. Personal testimony does not establish every inference that might be drawn from it. Negromancy requires these distinctions because its inquiry crosses several forms of knowing without reducing them to one another. The method is therefore interdisciplinary, but interdisciplinarity alone is not its distinguishing accomplishment. Its more specific ambition is to investigate how different forms of mediation establish particular conditions of appearance, recognition, authority, and action—and how these conditions affect what becomes possible. The October observation archive introduces an important discipline. An observation should remain distinguishable from the interpretation developed around it. An inference should remain open to contradiction. A concept should not become authoritative merely because it can be made to explain several unrelated examples. The framework must remain responsive to the encounters through which it is developed. This requires the practitioner to acknowledge the possibility that a particular object will resist, complicate, or invalidate a Negromantic interpretation. Theoretical coherence is not itself evidence that the world has been accurately described. A method that cannot be corrected by the relations it investigates would reproduce a central problem the framework seeks to examine: the elevation of a historically constituted interpretive system into an apparently sovereign account of reality.

XV. Prodigal Humanism and the question of the human

The earlier Wynterian inquiry into the historically specific production of Man continues to inform Negromancy's developing engagement with humanism. Prodigal Humanism has emerged as a possible Negromantic intervention into this problem, not as a separate governing philosophy. Its concern is the historical production and authorization of the human, especially where the displacement of divine authority has coincided with the establishment of new sovereign claims within the figure of Man. The question is not simply whether humanism has excluded certain populations from an otherwise adequate account of humanity. It is whether the categories through which humanity is recognized have themselves become detached from, or elevated above, the relations that constitute human life.

A Negromantic intervention would therefore investigate how particular genres of the human acquire authority, organize institutions, mediate moral recognition, and establish conditions under which lives become intelligible or expendable. But it would also question whether an alternative account of humanity can avoid repeating the drive toward an exhaustive definition of the human. Glissantian opacity remains important precisely here. The alternative to a historically exclusionary account of Man need not be another total description into which every form of life must fit. The possibility is a conception of human existence answerable to relational constitution without requiring that relation become fully transparent or that its possibilities be closed in advance. This remains an important direction rather than a completed theoretical result.

XVI. What Negromancy appears to be becoming

The preceding arguments suggest that Negromancy is developing toward a philosophical account of historically mediated existence and the cultivation of transformative capacity. Its starting point is relational rather than individualist. Its understanding of subjectivity is historical and embodied rather than self-originating. Its conception of history extends beyond completed events toward the ongoing constitution of present life and future possibility. Its account of knowledge investigates the mediations through which relations become perceptible, recognizable, and authoritative. Its understanding of power includes the organization of the conditions under which beings acquire or are denied capacities to recognize, act, and transform relations. Its ethics attends to the distribution of bearing and to the distinction between preserving coherence and repairing its conditions. Its practice depends upon encounter, historical inquiry, performance, testimony, rehearsal, and answerable intervention. Its epistemic limits are protected by distinctions among observation, inference, testimony, sacred interpretation, ethical judgment, and unresolved possibility. And its refusal of total possession is expressed through opacity and Mystery. These commitments do not yet establish an entirely coherent philosophical system. Several conceptual relationships require further development.

Unresolved theoretical tensions

1. Relation and ontology Does Negromancy make a claim about the fundamental constitution of reality, or primarily about the historical relations through which particular worlds become encounterable? The two claims must be distinguished. A theory of historically mediated perception does not, by itself, establish a general relational ontology.

2. Historical constitution and agency If bodily capacities and interpretive repertoires are historically constituted, what explains the emergence of responses that exceed established forms? Rehearsal provides one practical model, but it does not fully explain how genuinely new capacities or possibilities emerge.

3. Mediation and material causality A mediation can alter recognition without altering the underlying material process. In other cases, categorization or institutional recognition may causally change the relations being described. Negromancy needs a disciplined account of these differences.

4. Opacity and knowledge If all encounters remain incomplete, what distinguishes a productive recognition of explanatory limits from a failure to investigate sufficiently? Opacity must not become a means of protecting weak arguments from correction.

5. Spirit and causal explanation Negromancy must preserve the integrity of spiritual experience and historically situated sacred practices while maintaining appropriate standards for claims about causal mechanisms. No single vocabulary should automatically settle the status of every encounter.

6. Bearing and repair A transformation may redistribute burdens while producing new ones. How should competing obligations, unavoidable costs, and conflicting interests be evaluated? The refusal of sacrificial bearing establishes a powerful ethical orientation, but not a complete account of political decision-making.

7. Witness and authority Who may legitimately claim to witness, represent, or interpret a relation? What distinguishes developed capacities of discernment from claims of privileged access that reproduce domination? This becomes particularly important where sacred, scientific, legal, and political forms of authority intersect.

8. Historical specificity and general application Negromancy originates in Black-Caribbean historical experience. Its conceptual reach may extend beyond that experience, but doing so must not erase the histories that made the method necessary or imply that every society reproduces the same mechanisms.

9. Performance and explanation Performance can make relations available for investigation and may transform participants. It is not automatically an explanation of what produces them. Negromancy must continue distinguishing the epistemic and practical contributions of performance from the causal claims made about social and historical processes.

10. Theory and its own conditions of production Negromancy itself has emerged through historically situated bodies, conversations, scholarly traditions, artistic practices, technologies, and interpretive procedures. If the framework takes mediation seriously, it must remain capable of examining the conditions through which its own concepts become intelligible and authoritative. Its methods must therefore be available for revision under the same standards of critical attention that it brings to other systems.

XVII. A provisional synthesis

Negromancy is an emerging Black-Caribbean philosophy and practice concerned with the historical constitution, mediation, embodiment, and transformation of relations. It begins from the recognition that historically produced relations may remain active despite being obscured, violently disrupted, naturalized, or declared finished. It investigates how those relations participate in constituting worlds, forming subjects, organizing perception, establishing authority, distributing capacities and burdens, and making particular futures possible. Central to its inquiry is historical consciousness: the cultivated capacity to discern how embodied past and emerging future participate in present existence. Through research, performance, rehearsal, testimony, ritual, and other situated practices, Negromancy seeks to make historically active relations available for critical and embodied encounter while preserving the distinctions among experience, evidence, interpretation, and spiritual understanding. It treats opacity as a limit on exhaustive possession, Mystery as disciplined openness at explanatory limits, and bearing as a means of investigating the often unequal costs through which arrangements remain coherent. Its ethical orientation is reparative: not merely to reinterpret existing relations, but to cultivate and support capacities for changing the conditions through which harmful relations are reproduced. Negromancy's developing ambition is therefore to cultivate forms of historical consciousness capable of recognizing how worlds are constituted, discerning how their relations are sustained, and participating answerably in the formation of different possibilities of life. It does not claim that the world becomes fully knowable through this practice. Nor does it assume that recognition alone constitutes transformation. The practice remains unfinished because the relations it investigates, the capacities it cultivates, and the consequences of its interventions cannot be possessed in advance. Its obligation is to remain answerable to what the encounter reveals, what it does not reveal, and what becomes possible or impossible through the response.
