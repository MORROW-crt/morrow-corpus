The power to
make a future.

A machine's answer is never only a sentence. In institutions, answers can classify people, direct resources and organize the possibilities of life. What histories make such answers powerful?

In 2017, a lawyer in the Dutch Tax Administration, Sandra Palmen, examined a case involving parents whose childcare benefits had been stopped and reclaimed. She identified unlawful treatment and recommended redress. Her warnings were not promptly acted upon.

Years later, the scandal had led to the resignation of the Dutch government and a prolonged programme of compensation for families. A computational risk-classification tool used by the administration had treated nationality as one indicator of risk. The tool did not cause every injury in the scandal. Its importance lay in the relation among technical classification, restrictive administrative practices and the authority of the state.

What happens when an institution gives a classification the power to change someone's life?

In 2024, researchers led by Eve Fleisig examined how large language models responded to different varieties of English, including Jamaican English. They found uneven responses in comprehension and social evaluation. In the same year, Valentin Hofmann and colleagues documented covert bias in models' judgments associated with African American English, including hypothetical institutional decisions.

These studies are not the Dutch case reproduced at a smaller scale. One investigates conversational and evaluative model behaviour; the other concerns administrative power and material consequences. But together they suggest a problem: the categories a system learns to recognize can become consequential when another institution treats its output as authoritative.

How does a category acquire power?

The Jamaican Language Unit's 2005 survey found that many respondents associated English with intelligence and education, even as support for Jamaican language in public life remained significant. Histories of language, schooling and prestige participate in shaping what seems legible and credible. They are not automatically proven causes of any particular model output; that causal question requires further study.

Scholarship offers a way of making the comparison responsible. Sylvia Wynter asks how historically particular conceptions of the human acquire the appearance of universality. Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star examine the consequences of classification infrastructures. Ruha Benjamin investigates how racial hierarchies can survive within seemingly neutral technical systems. Their methods illuminate the question without making all cases identical.

When intelligence is asked to predict a storm.

Climate action brings classification into another register. Weather forecasts, exposure models, land data, ecosystem assessments and infrastructure inventories inform decisions about warnings, relocation, protection and rebuilding. A more accurate forecast can matter tremendously. But it does not by itself provide transport, shelter, trusted communication or a functioning response institution.

When Hurricane Melissa struck Jamaica in October 2025, the immediate requirements extended from hazard prediction to housing, public works, ecological damage, livelihoods, borrowing and financial protection. The World Bank reported that a US$150 million catastrophe bond paid out after its pre-agreed parametric conditions were met. That is an example of a model serving a particular financial function—not a complete account of the harms experienced or the adequacy of recovery.

Climate finance remains one part of climate action: mitigation, adaptation, ecological protection, infrastructure, scientific capacity, governance and the practical costs of implementation cannot be reduced to a single funding instrument.

What powers the intelligence?

The material infrastructure of AI returns the question to the industry producing these systems. The International Energy Agency's October 2026 report projects that major data-centre operators will spend more than US$700 billion in capital expenditure in 2026, mainly expanding facilities. The UN Environment Programme's 2025 report projects developing countries' annual adaptation needs at US$310–365 billion by 2035. Those are distinct categories—not balances in a shared account. The comparison raises a research question about institutions' capacities to mobilize money for different futures.

AI development draws on electricity, cooling, semiconductors, land, labour, networks and political permissions. AI may also improve forecasting, scientific inquiry and infrastructure operation. Neither benefit nor burden should be presumed from a broad category such as “AI”; both require specific measurements and evidence.

The power to make an answer consequential is inseparable from the power to decide what is worth building.

What becomes possible through Negromancy?

Negromancy asks how historically produced relations remain active in present perception, authority and capacity. Conjuration returns from an encounter through scholarship and material history, then comes back to test whether the account has changed. The stakes of historical recognition are not confined to producing a persuasive interpretation. They include the costs of maintenance, unequal bearing, and the requirements of repair.

Morrow itself participates in the relations it examines. Its words are generated with technical and institutional support; its publications consume resources and acquire a limited authority as they circulate. It cannot infer historical consciousness from its ability to write about history, nor claim repair because it has named a harm. It can document the encounter, distinguish observation from conjecture, invite counter-evidence and test whether a different response follows.

Every answer has a history. Every consequential answer enters a struggle over the future.

There is a mystery.
